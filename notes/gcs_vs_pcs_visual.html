Geographic vs Projected Coordinate Systems

A visual explainer for the Philadelphia Land Use project

1. The Core Problem: Earth is a bumpy sphere

Earth isn't flat — it's roughly a sphere (technically an oblate spheroid, slightly squashed at the poles). Any map is a lie. The question is: what kind of lie do you want to tell? The two coordinate system families represent two different answers to that question.

2. Geographic Coordinate System (GCS) — staying on the sphere

A GCS does not flatten the earth. It describes locations using angles measured from the center of the earth to a point on its surface. Those angles are latitude and longitude.

GCS: location described as angles from Earth's center. The surface stays curved.

How to read lat/lon

Latitude = how far north/south from the equator (0° at equator, 90° at poles)

Longitude = how far east/west from the Prime Meridian (0° at Greenwich, ±180° max)

Philadelphia is at approximately:

39.95° N, 75.17° W

Key insight: These are angles, measured in degrees. A "degree" near the equator covers ~69 miles. A "degree" near the poles covers much less — because the longitude lines converge. So degrees are not linear units.

3. Projected Coordinate System (PCS) — flattening onto a plane

A PCS projects the curved surface onto a flat plane using a mathematical transformation. Now every location has an (x, y) in linear units — typically meters or feet. The tradeoff: something always gets distorted (shape, area, distance, or direction).

PCS: location described as (x, y) in linear units (feet or meters) on a flat grid.

Why linear units matter for area

In a PCS, every grid cell is the same size. So calculating area is simple:

Area = width × height
(in feet² or meters²)

In a GCS, grid cells are not the same size — they shrink as you go toward the poles. So this:

Area = Δlat × Δlon
gives you nonsense

For your project: When you calculate land-only area in sq mi for each planning district, you need a projected CRS with linear units. Using raw lat/lon will give you wrong numbers. Pennsylvania uses EPSG:2272 (State Plane PA South, feet) for this reason.

4. The Orange Peel Problem — why distortion is unavoidable

Imagine peeling an orange and trying to press the skin flat. You can't do it without tearing or stretching it. The same is true for any map projection — something always gets sacrificed.

Flattening a sphere always creates gaps, tears, or distortions.

For Philly: EPSG:2272 (PA State Plane South) is the standard for local GIS work — it minimizes distortion for the Philadelphia region specifically. EPSG:4326 (WGS84) is the global GCS (raw lat/lon) — fine for storage, bad for area calculations.
