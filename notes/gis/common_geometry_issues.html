Common Geometry Validity Issues

Philadelphia Land Use Analytics — Task 6.7 visual supplement

Context: we're joining permit points (L&I data) onto planning district polygons using point-in-polygon spatial joins. Geometry validity issues in either layer break this process.

Issue 1 — Self-Intersecting Polygon (Bowtie)

Valid polygon

Simple closed ring — boundary never crosses itself. "Inside" is unambiguous.

Self-intersecting polygon (bowtie)

The boundary crosses itself. Is the center region "inside" or not? PostGIS can't determine containment reliably.

Project impact: If a planning district boundary has a self-intersection, PostGIS's ST_Within() may return NULL, FALSE, or throw an error for permits that fall in the ambiguous zone. Those permits go unassigned — silently dropped from district rollup counts.
Fix: ST_IsValid(geom) returns FALSE for self-intersecting geometries. ST_MakeValid(geom) attempts to repair them automatically (PostGIS 2.0+).

Issue 2 — Gaps Between Adjacent Polygons

No gap — clean boundary

Shared edge: every address falls in exactly one district.

Gap between districts

Permit address falls in the gap — not inside either district. Spatial join returns no match.

Project impact: Permits at addresses along district boundary gaps get dropped from all district rollups. This is especially risky for permits near known boundary revision areas or streets that run along district edges. Your monthly permit counts will undercount, and the undercounting is geographically non-random (concentrated at boundaries).
Detection: After the spatial join, count WHERE district_id IS NULL. If non-trivial, investigate addresses geographically — they'll cluster at boundary gaps. Document the rate in the limitations register.

Issue 3 — Overlapping Polygons (Double-Counting)

Overlapping districts

Permit in the overlap zone passes ST_Within() for both District A and B. It gets counted twice.

Why this is hard to catch

Overlaps in Philadelphia planning district boundaries are unlikely for the official city shapefile, but can appear when:

  • Boundary data was edited manually and a vertex was dragged inward
  • Different vintages of the shapefile are accidentally mixed
  • A simplification algorithm was applied and introduced micro-overlaps
Impact: One permit appears in two districts' counts. Aggregated totals will be > actual permit count. Off by a small amount but geographically biased toward boundary areas.
Check: After spatial join, GROUP BY permit_id HAVING COUNT(*) > 1. Any permit assigned to more than one district reveals an overlap.

Issue 4 — Duplicate Vertices

Two consecutive boundary points at the exact same coordinate — a zero-length edge in the polygon ring.

Impact vs. Self-Intersections

Duplicate vertices are the mildest validity issue:

IssueST_IsValid()?Breaks join?

The real risk of duplicate vertices is their bigger cousin — the self-intersecting polygon. A boundary that crosses itself creates ambiguous interior regions, which is what breaks point-in-polygon tests.
