Spatial Join Mechanics

This document explains the two spatial joins used in the Philadelphia LandUse Analytics MVP — how they work mechanically, what data they connect, and concrete examples of what the output looks like.

Join 1 — Permits (Points) → Planning Districts (Polygons)

Purpose: Assign each permit record to the planning district it falls within, so permit activity can be aggregated at the district level.

Join type: Point-in-polygon spatial join.

How it works

Each permit in the L&I dataset has a geocoded latitude/longitude coordinate. Each planning district is a polygon. The join tests whether each permit point falls inside any planning district polygon. If it does, the permit inherits that district's identifier.

Each colored point is a permit. Each permit is tested against all district polygons. The permit inherits the district it lands in.

Concrete example

Output grain: one row per permit, with district_id attached. Downstream, these are aggregated to (district_id, month, permit_type) for metric calculation.
Risk: Permits with missing or failed geocoding will not match any district polygon and will be dropped from district-level metrics. Track the unmatched share as a data quality metric.

Join 2 — Zoning Polygons → Planning Districts (Polygons)

Purpose: Compute what share of each planning district's land area belongs to each zoning classification, for each vintage year.

Join type: Polygon overlay (intersection), with area-weighted aggregation.

How it works

Zoning polygons and planning district polygons are overlaid. Where a zoning polygon crosses a district boundary, it is clipped to the intersection. The area of each clipped piece is calculated. These area pieces are then aggregated by (district_id, vintage_year, zoning_code) to compute the total land area of each zoning class per district per year.

Zoning polygons are clipped to district boundaries. Only the area inside the district counts. A polygon that crosses a boundary contributes area to both districts proportionally.

Concrete example — area aggregation

After intersection, the raw output looks like:

Churn detection: In this example, 150,000 sq ft of land shifted from ICMX to CMX-4 between 2022 and 2023 — detectable by comparing the two vintage years side by side. This is the year-to-year zoning churn metric.
Risk — slivers: Tiny intersection fragments from polygon boundary misalignment across vintages can appear as false zoning changes. Apply a minimum area threshold (e.g., ignore intersections < 500 sq ft) before computing churn metrics.
